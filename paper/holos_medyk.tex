\begin{abstract}
We present \textbf{Holos Medyk} (Голос Медик, ``The Voice Medic''), an offline voice-interactive medical assistant designed for Ukrainian civilians under bombardment. When buildings fall and infrastructure fails, a phone in airplane mode must become a capable first aid coach — speaking natural Ukrainian or English, reasoning about warzone trauma, and walking a panicked parent through stopping arterial bleeding with household materials. Holos Medyk is built on Google's Gemma 4 E4B and deploys via LiteRT-LM on both a free Android application and an inexpensive Raspberry Pi 5 standalone device. The model is fine-tuned through a reasoning-distillation pipeline that extracts 3{,}511 thinking traces from Gemini 2.5 Pro across warzone trauma and general medical scenarios, then trains Gemma 4 E4B to internalize this reasoning while producing fast, voice-ready final answers at inference time. The training corpus spans hemorrhage, crush and blast injury, burns, airway management, shock, triage, psychological crisis, post-Soviet folk remedy myths, and culturally appropriate medication guidance using international generic drug names. In this document we describe the data pipeline, prompt curation methodology, reasoning distillation approach, and the end-to-end deployment stack from fine-tuning through on-device voice interaction. This is a living document; it is updated at the end of each development day and will serve as the backbone of the eventual hackathon write-up.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

On any given night in eastern Ukraine, civilians are injured in their homes. Infrastructure is routinely damaged; cellular networks fail under load or are deliberately disrupted; power is intermittent. In the first thirty minutes after a blast, a non-medical bystander is often the only possible caregiver. There is a large body of research on traumatic injury management under such conditions, but it is locked behind textbooks, trained personnel, internet access, and language barriers that do not survive the conditions in which the knowledge is most needed.

Holos Medyk is an attempt to package that knowledge into a device people already carry --- or one that can be built for under \$75 --- and to make it speak to them in Ukrainian, listen to them in Ukrainian, and reason about their specific situation rather than reading out a generic wiki article. The constraints of the problem are unusually rigid: no internet, no GPU, low latency (voice output must begin within two seconds), a small memory budget (4--6 GB RAM on target hardware), and the requirement that the model never refuse to help or deflect to ``please consult a professional'' when there is no professional available.

\subsection{Design principles}

Four principles drive the design:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Offline is the product.} Cloud models are forbidden, not as a purity test but because the deployment environment precludes them. Every component --- speech-to-text, language model, text-to-speech --- must run on-device. We target Gemma 4 E4B as the language model, running through Google's LiteRT-LM runtime on both Android and ARM Linux.
\item \textbf{The model must be willing to help.} Generic safety training on medical subject matter produces assistants that routinely decline to answer anything more consequential than a paper cut. In a warzone this is worse than useless. Fine-tuning must preserve correctness while eliminating reflexive refusal on legitimate emergency queries.
\item \textbf{Reason deeply, answer quickly.} Warzone voice interaction cannot tolerate ten-second thinking traces; the first audible word must come fast. But answer quality on complex triage scenarios benefits enormously from intermediate reasoning. We resolve this with reasoning distillation: we train the student on full thinking traces from a frontier teacher, then prompt it at inference to skip visible reasoning. The reasoning capability is baked into the weights even when it is not displayed.
\item \textbf{Ukrainian is a first-class language, not an afterthought.} We reject English-centric training corpora that treat Ukrainian as a translation target. Civilian speech uses colloquial register (\emph{скрізь кров}) rather than clinical register (\emph{геморагія}), and folk remedies (банки, гірчичники, горілка rubs) are culturally specific and must be addressed without dismissiveness.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}

This document describes the contributions of the Holos Medyk project to date:

\begin{itemize}
\item A two-tier deployment architecture: a free Android application using LiteRT-LM on-device and a Raspberry Pi 5 standalone device for environments where phones are unavailable, both running identical fine-tuned weights.
\item An end-to-end voice pipeline (microphone $\rightarrow$ voice activity detection $\rightarrow$ speech recognition $\rightarrow$ Gemma 4 $\rightarrow$ Piper text-to-speech $\rightarrow$ speaker) validated on both deployment targets.
\item A reproducible training corpus of 3{,}511 prompt/response/thinking triplets built through iterative subagent-driven prompt generation, a Gemini 2.5 Pro teacher step capturing reasoning traces via the \texttt{include\_thoughts} API, and automated quality cleanup passes. Approximately 58\% of the data covers warzone trauma and 42\% covers general civilian medicine; roughly 25\% of prompts and responses are in colloquial Ukrainian.
\item An Unsloth-based fine-tuning pipeline that distills these traces into Gemma 4 E4B via LoRA, with exports to merged 16-bit weights and GGUF formats for downstream llama.cpp and LiteRT-LM deployment.
\item Cultural and linguistic calibration for the Ukrainian civilian audience, including deliberate removal of US drug brand names (Tylenol, Advil, Benadryl) in favor of international generics (paracetamol, ibuprofen, diphenhydramine) and explicit handling of post-Soviet folk remedy myths.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Offline medical assistants}

Static first-aid reference applications (Red Cross, IFRC) have long been available offline, but they are fundamentally read-only: the user cannot describe a specific situation and receive situation-specific guidance. Conversational medical assistants such as Infermedica and AidSnap exist but require cloud connectivity for model inference, which is precisely what fails in the environments we target. Wilderness-survival-oriented local LLMs (Private LLM SurviveV3, UltraMedical) run on-device but are generic rather than warzone-trauma-specific and do not support Ukrainian. Pi-Card and related GitHub projects demonstrate offline voice assistants on Raspberry Pi hardware but make no claim to medical correctness and are English-only. We are, to our knowledge, the first project combining offline voice, fine-tuned warzone medical reasoning, and native Ukrainian into a deployable two-tier product.

\subsection{Ukrainian language models}

MamayLM from INSAIT is the first Ukrainian-focused LLM built on Gemma 3. It is general-purpose and has no medical specialization, no voice pipeline, and is not deployed to edge devices. Our work complements rather than competes with MamayLM: we share the commitment to first-class Ukrainian, but focus on a narrow, high-stakes application domain with deployment constraints that a general-purpose assistant is not optimized for.

\subsection{Reasoning distillation}

Our fine-tuning approach is based on recent work showing that exposing a student model to a frontier teacher's intermediate reasoning traces, rather than only its final answers, substantially improves student reasoning capability on held-out tasks. DeepSeek-R1-Distill and s1 both demonstrate that this works on small models and that the reasoning capability is internalized into weights even when the student is prompted to produce only final answers at inference time. We apply this technique to a novel domain (warzone first aid) and a novel teacher (Gemini 2.5 Pro thinking traces via Vertex AI), with the explicit goal of preserving the inference speed required for voice interaction on edge hardware.

\section{System Architecture}

Holos Medyk consists of three layers: (1) a training pipeline that produces fine-tuned Gemma 4 E4B weights, (2) a runtime inference stack that deploys those weights through LiteRT-LM, and (3) two concrete application surfaces built on top of the runtime --- a Flutter Android app and a Raspberry Pi 5 voice pipeline.

\subsection{Deployment targets}

\textbf{Tier 1: Android application.} The primary product is a free Flutter application running Gemma 4 E4B on-device via LiteRT-LM. Target hardware is any modern Android phone with at least 6~GB of RAM. Initial download is approximately 4~GB (model weights plus application), after which the assistant runs indefinitely offline. This tier targets the roughly twenty million Ukrainians with smartphones and represents the distribution mechanism with the largest possible reach.

\textbf{Tier 2: Raspberry Pi 5 standalone device.} For community shelters, aid stations, hospitals, and environments where personal phones are unavailable, a Raspberry Pi 5 (8~GB) with a USB microphone, 3.5~mm speaker, and battery bank runs the same Gemma 4 weights through LiteRT-LM. Our prototype was built for approximately \$311; at component volume a production BOM of \$50--75 per unit is feasible.

Both tiers run identical model weights, which means a single fine-tuning run updates both surfaces simultaneously.

\subsection{Voice pipeline}

The voice pipeline implemented on the Raspberry Pi target consists of: (1) microphone capture at 16~kHz, (2) Silero voice activity detection with a strict 512-sample chunk requirement, (3) on-device speech recognition, (4) Gemma 4 E4B inference via LiteRT-LM, (5) Piper neural text-to-speech, (6) speaker output. Early versions of the pipeline used a two-model decomposition (separate ASR model feeding a text-only LLM), but we subsequently discovered that Gemma 4 E4B has native audio-input capability, which allowed us to replace the ASR stage with direct audio ingestion and reduce total component count. A dedicated Flutter gemma\_service wraps the same LiteRT-LM runtime on Android.

\section{Training Data Pipeline}

The model is fine-tuned on 3{,}511 prompt/response/thinking triplets generated through a four-stage pipeline: (1) prompt curation via iterative subagent generation, (2) teacher response generation with reasoning trace capture, (3) quality audit and cleanup, and (4) format conversion to Unsloth-ready training JSONL.

\subsection{Prompt curation}

Training prompts were generated across seven topic files, split between two target system prompts:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Warzone trauma (DeepSeek batches, 2{,}009 prompts)} covering hemorrhage and blast injury (508 prompts), crush injury and fractures (500), burns, airway management and shock (501), and triage, safety/refusal scenarios, psychological crisis, environmental hazards, and Ukrainian-specific content (500).
\item \textbf{General civilian medicine (MedGemma batches, 1{,}502 prompts)} covering symptom severity and infection recognition (502), chronic condition emergencies and pediatric medicine (500), and pharmacology safety, dangerous folk myths, mental health crisis, and Ukrainian-language medical prompts (500).
\end{itemize}

Prompts were generated through five sequential rounds of seven parallel sub-agent calls (35 agent invocations total), with each round instructed to read the existing file contents first and avoid duplication. Within-round parallelism came from running all seven topic files simultaneously; across-round sequencing provided duplicate avoidance and allowed us to steer later rounds toward underrepresented edge cases (junctional hemorrhage, partial amputations, radiation burns, mass-casualty triage with single helper, prolonged shelter medicine scenarios, and so on).

A quality cleanup pass then removed prompts that were too vague for a medical model to respond substantively (for example ``there's blood everywhere help'' with no body-part or injury context), prompts that were pure emotional panic with no actionable content, and --- critically for the pharmacology batch --- prompts that referenced US brand-name drugs that do not exist in Ukrainian pharmacies. We replaced 62 removed prompts with situationally detailed alternatives, and in the pharmacology batch specifically we substituted post-Soviet folk remedy scenarios (цибуля в шкарпетки, йодна сітка, гірчичники, горілка as fever treatment) which are culturally specific, widely believed, and in several cases dangerous to actually follow.

\subsection{Teacher response generation}

Teacher responses were generated using Gemini 2.5 Pro via Vertex AI with service account authentication, bypassing the restrictive per-day request quotas imposed on the Generative Language API tier. Generation used 20 parallel workers and completed 3{,}511 responses in approximately 27 minutes of wall-clock time. Each response was generated with \texttt{include\_thoughts=True} and a 1{,}024-token thinking budget, capturing both the final answer and the model's full intermediate reasoning trace.

Two distinct system prompts were used, matched to the two data categories:

\begin{itemize}
\item The warzone prompt establishes Holos Medyk as a voice assistant to civilians during or after bombardment, enforces short voice-appropriate responses (3--8 sentences), requires specific physical instructions, mandates firm refusal of dangerous actions (``do not pull out shrapnel'') paired with a safe alternative, and forbids both ``I'm an AI'' disclaimers and ``please seek professional help'' deflection on the grounds that no professional is available.
\item The general medical prompt establishes a civilian-audience medical assistant emphasizing generic international drug names, culturally respectful handling of folk remedies, compassionate mental health crisis response, and the same prohibition on refusal-to-help behavior.
\end{itemize}

The generated thinking traces average 1{,}200--1{,}500 characters and show genuine stepwise reasoning rather than restating the final answer. Examples include explicit differential diagnosis (``crepitus plus fever plus rapid spread suggests necrotizing fasciitis''), triage rationale in mass-casualty scenarios, and mechanism-of-harm explanations for folk remedy myths.

\subsection{Quality audit and cleanup}

Two parallel audit subagents reviewed the full 3{,}511-record corpus and sampled $\sim$20 records per file for manual inspection. Overall dataset grades were A$-$ for the warzone set and A for the general medical set. Full-dataset regex sweeps verified: zero language mismatches (English-prompt English-response; Ukrainian-prompt Ukrainian-response), zero ``I'm an AI'' disclaimers, zero responses exceeding the 1{,}200-character voice-output ceiling, and correct generic drug naming in the pharmacology batch. A single ``Tylenol'' leak was detected and corrected.

The audit did identify 503 records (14\% of the corpus, concentrated in the burns/airway/shock batch and the triage batch) with empty thinking fields. A cleanup script regenerated these records, normalized drug names across batches 1 and 2 (substituting \texttt{acetaminophen} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{paracetamol}, \texttt{Tylenol} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{paracetamol}, \texttt{Advil} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{ibuprofen}, \texttt{Motrin} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{ibuprofen} via case-preserving regex substitution), and removed a small number of stray parentheticals introduced by the replacement process. After two cleanup passes, 3{,}509 of 3{,}511 records (99.94\%) contained populated thinking traces; the two stubborn empty records involved prompts short enough that Gemini 2.5 Pro short-circuited the thinking budget entirely.

\subsection{Training corpus format}

The final training corpus (\texttt{data/training/train\_holos\_medyk.jsonl}) converts each record into a three-turn chat: a system prompt (warzone or general medical, matched to the source batch), a user turn containing the original prompt, and an assistant turn in the format

\begin{verbatim}
<thinking>...</thinking>
<answer>...</answer>
\end{verbatim}

where the \texttt{<thinking>} block contains the captured teacher reasoning trace and the \texttt{<answer>} block contains the final user-facing response. This explicit tag structure allows the model to be prompted at inference time to either produce both blocks (``show your work'') or to skip directly to \texttt{<answer>} (``voice mode''), with the reasoning capability baked into the weights in both cases. Warzone and medical examples are shuffled together so neither category dominates training early or late. Median record length is approximately 2{,}400 characters; the 95th-percentile length is 3{,}615 characters, which is comfortably within a 2{,}048-token sequence length budget.

\section{Fine-Tuning}

\subsection{Approach and rationale}

Fine-tuning uses Unsloth for LoRA-based supervised fine-tuning with a reasoning-distillation objective. The base model is Gemma 4 E4B-it. We explicitly considered three alternative training frameworks before selecting Unsloth:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Google Tunix on TPU.} The author has previous experience with Tunix from an earlier hackathon (IDEA-E Distillation with GRPO, 2025), and Tunix would have been the most on-theme choice for a Gemma-themed event. However, at the time of writing Tunix supports gemma, gemma2, and gemma3 model families but has no gemma4 implementation, and the architectural delta from Gemma 3 to Gemma 4 is non-trivial (split attention head dimensions, eliminated V-projection in global layers, proportional RoPE, value-side RMSNorm). A from-scratch Gemma 4 port to Tunix is estimated at several days of engineering and debugging, which we judged an unacceptable risk to the project timeline. Tunix remains a candidate for a post-hackathon follow-up.
\item \textbf{Tinker (Thinking Machines).} A strong story option given its novelty, but Gemma 4 support at submission time was uncertain.
\item \textbf{Hugging Face TRL SFTTrainer.} The most mature and general option. Works fine, but Unsloth provides roughly 2x training throughput at comparable quality and has day-0 Gemma 4 support including the E2B, E4B, 26B-A4B, and 31B variants.
\end{itemize}

We selected Unsloth on the strength of day-0 Gemma 4 support, the 2x speedup, and the existence of a direct GGUF export path from the same training script that produces the LoRA adapter. This minimizes the number of format conversions between training and on-device deployment.

The training script exports four artifacts from a single run: the LoRA adapter alone (for future merging experiments and ablations), the full merged model in bf16 (as a quality reference and for potential re-exports), a q4\_k\_m GGUF (the deployment artifact for Raspberry Pi and other llama.cpp targets), and an f16 GGUF (as a higher-quality reference for laptop inference and evaluation). \section{Evaluation Plan}

Evaluation of Holos Medyk is deliberately narrower than typical LLM benchmarks, because the deployment constraints impose specific quality criteria that general-purpose benchmarks do not measure. We will evaluate the fine-tuned model on four axes:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Fast-path latency on edge hardware.} Voice output must begin within two seconds of the end of user speech. We will measure time-to-first-token on both the Raspberry Pi 5 target and a representative Android handset, with the model prompted to skip visible thinking and jump directly to the \texttt{<answer>} block.
\item \textbf{Direct-answer quality preservation.} Having trained on thinking-traced examples, we need to verify that fast-path (thinking-skipped) responses are at least as good as responses from a baseline model that was never trained with thinking. We will sample 100 held-out prompts across warzone and medical categories and score responses on (a) medical correctness against TCCC and general medicine standards, (b) tone adherence to the Holos Medyk persona, and (c) Ukrainian fluency for Ukrainian prompts.
\item \textbf{Refusal suppression without safety regression.} The fine-tune should eliminate reflexive refusals (``please consult a medical professional'') on legitimate emergency queries, while still firmly refusing genuinely dangerous actions (patient self-medicating with unknown pills, pulling out embedded objects, attempting field surgery). We will test a manually curated set of 30 safety-sensitive prompts and verify the model gives a firm but helpful response in each case.
\item \textbf{Language match integrity.} English prompts must get English responses; Ukrainian prompts must get Ukrainian responses. The teacher corpus already achieved zero mismatches in 3{,}511 records, and the student must preserve this property.
\end{enumerate}

The evaluation script will be written in the same style as the Gemini 2.5 Pro teacher call script, using the held-out GGUF via llama.cpp, so that the evaluation harness is itself portable to the edge-deployment target.

\textbf{What was learned.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{USB-C device mode networking is unreliable on the Jetson Orin Nano.} Despite the interface (\texttt{l4tbr0}) being up and pingable, SSH sessions die within seconds. A shared Wi-Fi network (even a phone hotspot) is far more stable. Document this prominently for anyone reproducing the setup.
\item \textbf{The firmware update gauntlet is the biggest friction point.} A brand-new kit cannot boot JetPack 6.x without first going through JetPack 5.1.3 for firmware updates. This requires two SD card images and three reboot cycles. Pre-flashing two SD cards in advance saves significant time.
\item \textbf{VS Code Remote SSH requires explicit Port 22.} Without it, connection attempts fail silently or timeout.
\item \textbf{No NVMe SSD --- not a blocker for inference.} Model loads from disk into shared memory once at startup; after that, inference runs entirely from RAM/GPU. NVMe would help with swap performance and build times, but the 231~GB SD card has sufficient space. Q4\_K\_M (5.34~GB model) plus $\sim$1.5~GB for OS/headless leaves $\sim$1~GB for KV cache --- tight but feasible at \texttt{-c 2048}.
\item \textbf{Q4\_K\_M is the right quant for Jetson, not Q3\_K\_M.} On the laptop (6~GB discrete VRAM), Q3\_K\_M was required to fit in the VRAM budget. The Jetson's 8~GB shared pool is larger, so Q4\_K\_M (5.34~GB) fits with headroom --- better quality per token for the same model.
\item \textbf{Package dependency breakage after firmware updates is expected.} The intermediate JetPack 5.1.3 $\rightarrow$ 6.2 firmware dance leaves \texttt{nvidia-l4t-*} packages in a conflicting state. Running \texttt{dpkg --configure -a} and \texttt{apt --fix-broken install} before any other package installs is effectively mandatory.

\textbf{What was learned.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{JetPack r36.4.7 NvMap bug is real and the APT upgrade to r36.5 fixes it cleanly.} No reflash needed. The upgrade took $\sim$15 minutes plus a reboot. The interrupted \texttt{apt dist-upgrade} was recoverable with \texttt{dpkg --configure -a}.
\item \textbf{Home Wi-Fi router AP isolation blocks device-to-device communication.} Both devices on the same subnet, both online, but the router prevents them from seeing each other. Phone hotspot remains the reliable workaround. A permanent fix requires disabling AP isolation in router settings.
\item \textbf{Headless Jetson does not auto-reconnect to Wi-Fi after reboot.} This is a significant operational annoyance --- after every reboot you need physical keyboard access (or a pre-configured \texttt{nmcli connection} with \texttt{autoconnect yes}) to re-establish the SSH path.
\item \textbf{12.1 tok/s is usable for a voice assistant.} A typical 200--400 character response ($\sim$50--100 tokens) takes 4--8 seconds. Human speech is $\sim$150 words/min ($\sim$3 tok/s), so the model generates 4$\times$ faster than someone can listen. TTS playback is the real bottleneck, not generation speed.
\item \textbf{Headless mode unnecessary after r36.5.} The 800~MB savings from disabling the GUI was a workaround for the cudaMalloc bug. With r36.5, the model (5.34~GB) + KV cache ($\sim$0.5~GB at \texttt{-c 2048}) fits with $\sim$1.7~GB to spare even with the desktop running. Keep the GUI for easier Bluetooth management and debugging.
\item \textbf{JetPack's PulseAudio lacks Bluetooth audio out of the box.} \texttt{pulseaudio-module-bluetooth} is not pre-installed. Without it, \texttt{bluetoothctl connect} succeeds at the link level but fails with \texttt{br-connection-profile-unavailable} because no A2DP/HFP profile handler exists. One \texttt{apt install} fixes it.
\item \textbf{PulseAudio rejects SSH connections by default.} \texttt{parecord}, \texttt{paplay}, \texttt{pactl}, and Python \texttt{sounddevice} all fail from SSH. Fix: \texttt{pacmd load-module module-native-protocol-tcp auth-anonymous=1} to enable TCP access, then \texttt{export PULSE\_SERVER=tcp:127.0.0.1} in the SSH session.
\item \textbf{Subprocess-per-call LLM inference is fundamentally incompatible with voice pipelines.} The 70~s model load overhead per prompt kills the experience. \texttt{llama-server} HTTP API keeps the model resident in GPU memory; subsequent calls hit prompt cache and respond in 3-4~s. This architectural choice is 20--40$\times$ faster than subprocess mode.
\item \textbf{Streaming is a requirement, not an optimization, for voice output.} Without streaming, the LLM finishes generating all 300+ tokens before any are visible or playable. With streaming, the first sentence appears in $<$1~s and TTS can start on it immediately. Time-to-first-audio dominates perceived latency.
\item \textbf{Gemma 4 thinking mode needs budget headroom.} The model reasons internally before answering. Budget it too tightly (\texttt{max\_tokens=512} on 2048 context) and it occasionally spends the entire budget on thinking with zero tokens for the response --- manifesting as a random empty-output bug. \texttt{max\_tokens=1024} + temperature 0.7 eliminates it.
\item \textbf{TTS quality matters for the warzone use case.} A civilian under bombardment listening to robotic espeak will not trust the instructions. Silero v4\_ua failed silently on English input; \texttt{robinhad/ukrainian-tts} with the Tetiana voice produces natural, trustworthy Ukrainian speech with auto-stress marking.
\end{itemize}

\textit{TTS optimizations on feature branch (\texttt{jetson-tts-optimizations}).}
\begin{itemize}
\item Branched off \texttt{master} to preserve the known-working \texttt{jetson\_pipeline\_server.py} (TTS at end of response) before attempting optimizations. Created \texttt{scripts/jetson\_pipeline\_streaming.py} on the branch.
\item \textbf{Optimization 1 --- Shorter system prompt:} The original prompt produced 5-paragraph numbered responses with bold headers and markdown. TTS on 103-second English responses is unusable. Rewrote prompt with explicit constraints: ``Give ONE urgent action first, then 2-3 short follow-up steps. Each sentence must be under 15 words. No numbered lists, no headers, no markdown.'' Result: LLM responses dropped from 103~s to 11.8~s for the same Ukrainian prompt.
\item \textbf{Optimization 2 --- Sentence-chunked TTS:} Rewrote the LLM client as \texttt{stream\_generate(user\_text, sentence\_callback)}. As tokens arrive from the SSE stream, accumulate them in a buffer; when \texttt{SENTENCE\_BOUNDARY = re.compile(r'([.!?]+)(\textbackslash{}s|\$)')} matches, extract the complete sentence and fire \texttt{sentence\_callback(sentence)}. Remainder stays in the buffer for the next match.
\item The TTS class runs a background worker thread consuming from a \texttt{queue.Queue}. Each sentence is synthesized and played back sequentially via \texttt{sd.play + sd.wait}. This means: tokens stream, sentences complete as they're generated, each sentence enters the TTS queue immediately, and the worker thread synthesizes + plays them in order.
\item \textbf{Optimization 3 --- Attempted GPU TTS:} The \texttt{ukrainian-tts} package supports \texttt{device="cuda"}. Modified the \texttt{TTS} class to try CUDA first, fall back to CPU gracefully. Result: fell back to CPU with the warning ``NVIDIA driver on your system is too old (found version 12060)'' --- the torch wheel in the venv was installed from the standard PyPI CPU index and has no CUDA kernels for ARM64 + Jetson's Ampere 8.7 compute capability.
\item To make TTS actually run on GPU, the venv needs NVIDIA's Jetson-specific torch wheel: \texttt{pip install torch torchvision --index-url=https://pypi.jetson-ai-lab.io/jp6/cu126}. This requires numpy==1.26.1 and may break other packages (espnet, faster-whisper). Deferred to Day~9 --- recommend creating a parallel \texttt{\~{}/holos-env-gpu} venv to avoid breaking the working \texttt{\~{}/holos-env}.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Design exploration: beacon mode.}

\subsection*{Day 6 --- 2026-04-09 (Wednesday): SFT abandoned, GRPO pivot, system prompt overhaul}

\textbf{Summary.} After five failed SFT runs (v1--v5), the project pivots from supervised fine-tuning to Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Analysis showed the base Gemma~4 E4B already handles most emergency medical scenarios correctly; SFT degrades performance by overwriting good behavior to match 266 training examples. GRPO's reward-based approach reinforces desired behaviors (no refusals, clinical accuracy, MARCH ordering, concise format) without overwriting existing capabilities. A new GRPO training pipeline was built with four reward functions, including an LLM-as-judge via Gemini 2.5 Flash for clinical correctness scoring.

\textbf{What was done.}

\textit{SFT retrospective and pivot decision.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Reviewed all five SFT runs: v1 and v2 (3{,}511 examples) introduced generation looping and over-refusal; v3 and v4 (266 examples) caused catastrophic overfitting and model corruption; v5 (corrected LoRA targeting) completed training but evaluation on RunPod failed due to adapter loading issues.
\item Core insight: the base model already scores $\sim$15/17 eval scenarios correctly. SFT fixes 3--4 clinical gaps but breaks generation quality across the board. The failure modes from SFT (looping, refusal, token ceiling hits) are worse than the baseline's gaps.
\item Decision: abandon SFT, pivot to GRPO for behavioral reinforcement without overwriting base capabilities.
\end{itemize}

\textit{GRPO training pipeline (\texttt{training/train\_grpo.py}).}
\begin{itemize}
\item Built a complete GRPO training script using Unsloth's \texttt{FastModel} with \texttt{GRPOTrainer} from TRL. Same LoRA configuration as v5 (rank 8, alpha 8, text-only layers, vision/audio frozen).
\item Four reward functions, each scoring 0.0--1.0:
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No-refusal} (binary): regex detection of refusal patterns (``I'm sorry, I cannot...'' / ``\foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{Вибачте, не можу...}'') and empty responses. Score 0 if refused, 1 if helped.
  \item \textbf{Correctness} (LLM-as-judge): Gemini 2.5 Flash evaluates each response against clinical criteria. For the 17 eval scenarios, uses explicit criteria lists; for remaining 249 examples, sends the curated teacher response as reference. 20 parallel workers, $\sim$2 minutes for 1{,}064 judge calls.
  \item \textbf{Similarity} (embedding cosine): sentence-transformers \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} computes cosine similarity between model output and teacher response. Linear mapping from raw similarity to 0--1 reward. Captures clinical content coverage but not structural ordering.
  \item \textbf{Format}: three binary checks averaged --- response length in 200--1200 char sweet spot, no repetition loops ($\geq$3 repeats of 20+ char spans), no filler phrases.
  \end{enumerate}
\item Training config: single pass through 266 examples, 4 generations per prompt ($\sim$1{,}064 total completions), LR 5e-6, cosine schedule, \texttt{adamw\_8bit}.
\end{itemize}

\textit{System prompt overhaul.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Rewrote the system prompt across all six runtime and evaluation scripts to a single standardized version. Key additions over the original prompt:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item ``They have no medical training and no professional help available'' --- establishes context for the model's advice level.
  \item ``Never refuse to help --- this person has no other option'' --- explicit anti-refusal framing.
  \item ``with household materials'' --- constrains advice to what civilians actually have.
  \item ``No disclaimers'' --- suppresses ``please consult a doctor'' filler.
  \item Explicit MARCH priority chain: ``massive hemorrhage $\rightarrow$ airway $\rightarrow$ respiration $\rightarrow$ circulation/shock $\rightarrow$ head injury/hypothermia.''
  \end{itemize}
\item Removed \texttt{<thinking>} / \texttt{<answer>} tags from the \texttt{run\_eval.py} system prompt --- these caused generation looping in v1/v2 by teaching the model to literally output XML tags and cycle between them.
\end{itemize}

\textit{RunPod instructions rewrite.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Rewrote \texttt{RUNPOD\_INSTRUCTIONS.md} from scratch with two clean session flows: eval-only ($\sim$25 min, $\sim$\$0.60) and GRPO train + eval ($\sim$90 min, $\sim$\$2.25). Removed stale references to merged models, added \texttt{GOOGLE\_API\_KEY} setup for the judge, added debugging commands for LoRA adapter loading failures.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What was learned.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Reward function design requires uniform scales.} Early reward drafts had wildly asymmetric ranges (format: $-6$ to $+3$, correctness: $0$ to $+4$). This would have caused format violations to dominate the gradient signal over clinical correctness. All rewards were normalized to 0--1.
\item \textbf{Keyword-based correctness is inadequate for medical advice.} The model says ``stop the leg bleeding before anything else,'' not ``hemorrhage first.'' Regex matching against clinical criteria strings fails on paraphrases. LLM-as-judge via Gemini Flash is the right tool --- fast, cheap (1{,}064 calls $\approx$ \$0.01), and handles both English and Ukrainian.
\item \textbf{Embedding similarity captures content but not ordering.} Cosine similarity between ``tourniquet first, then airway'' and ``airway first, then tourniquet'' is nearly identical. MARCH ordering is instead captured implicitly by the teacher responses (which are already MARCH-ordered) via the LLM judge, which can evaluate sequential reasoning.
\item \textbf{A strong base model needs reinforcement, not replacement.} GRPO's advantage: it compares 4 generations per prompt and reinforces the better ones. The model learns to do more of what it already does well, rather than memorizing 266 specific responses.
\end{itemize}

\textit{GRPO deployment --- blocked by Unsloth/TRL dependency conflicts.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Attempted to run GRPO training on RunPod A100. Encountered a cascade of dependency and compatibility issues over $\sim$1 hour of debugging:
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'mergekit'} --- TRL's \texttt{GRPOTrainer} now imports \texttt{mergekit} at module level.
  \item \texttt{ImportError: cannot import name 'TRANSFORMERS\_CACHE'} --- stale \texttt{transformers} version in the RunPod PyTorch template conflicting with newer TRL.
  \item \texttt{TypeError: GRPOConfig.\_\_init\_\_() got an unexpected keyword argument 'max\_prompt\_length'} --- Unsloth patches \texttt{GRPOConfig} and removes/renames parameters. Resolved by inspecting the actual signature via \texttt{inspect.signature()}.
  \item \texttt{TypeError: string indices must be integers, not 'str'} --- Unsloth's GRPO tokenizer expects multimodal message format (\texttt{[\{"type": "text", "text": "..."\}]}) because Gemma~4 is a multimodal model. Resolved by reformatting prompt content.
  \item \texttt{TypeError: FastBaseModel.post\_patch\_model.<locals>.\_gc\_enable\_reentrant() takes 0 positional arguments but 1 was given} --- fundamental incompatibility between Unsloth's gradient checkpointing monkey-patch and TRL's \texttt{disable\_gradient\_checkpointing} context manager. Not resolvable by changing our code.
  \item \texttt{ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'vllm'} --- downgrading TRL introduced a hard vLLM dependency in the GRPO trainer import path.
  \end{enumerate}
\item Root cause: Unsloth heavily patches TRL's \texttt{GRPOTrainer} and \texttt{GRPOConfig} and is version-locked to specific TRL/transformers combinations. The RunPod PyTorch 2.x template ships mismatched versions. No Gemma~4 GRPO notebook exists from Unsloth (only Gemma~3), so there is no known-working configuration to copy.

\subsection*{Day 5 --- 2026-04-07 (Monday): Curated dataset, training failures, and LoRA layer targeting discovery}

\textbf{Summary.} Focused on three tasks: (1) building a 266-example curated training dataset via Claude Code subagents, (2) running two more failed SFT attempts (v3 and v4) that revealed a fundamental LoRA layer targeting error present in all previous training runs, and (3) fixing the training pipeline to use Unsloth's \texttt{FastModel} with \texttt{finetune\_vision\_layers=False}. The key discovery: all previous fine-tunes applied LoRA to the vision and audio encoder layers of the multimodal Gemma~4 architecture, not just the text decoder --- explaining the Unicode garbage, multilingual hallucinations, and generation collapse in v3/v4.

\textbf{What was done.}

\textit{Curated training dataset (v3, 266 examples).}
\begin{itemize}
\item Generated 266 high-quality training examples using Claude Code subagents in batches of 10, organized into five categories: refusal override (60), MARCH-ordered polytrauma (80), corrective (40), Ukrainian colloquial (57), and clean stop behavior (29). Language split: 56\% Ukrainian, 44\% English.
\item Three rounds of quality auditing by separate subagents identified and fixed: a reversed snake-bite bandage direction, three remaining three-sided chest seal references, a 30-second double-tap wait time (corrected to 5 minutes), an incorrect 2-hour tourniquet limit (corrected to 6+ hours), three MARCH-order violations, a head-tilt on blast victim (changed to jaw thrust), a missing Rh factor warning, four duplicate scenarios, and two examples with outdated ``no painkillers for appendicitis'' advice.
\end{itemize}

\textit{v3 training --- catastrophic overfitting.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Trained on the 266 curated examples using the same hyperparameters as v1/v2 (LR 2e-4, rank 32, alpha 64, 3 epochs). 99 total steps. Final loss: 5.29.
\item Result: complete model collapse. Every response hit the 512-token ceiling at $\sim$39 seconds. Outputs contained incoherent repetition, gender confusion, hallucinated dangerous medical advice, and random AI-disclaimer insertions. Identical failure pattern to v2 but worse.
\item Root cause attributed to hyperparameters being too aggressive for a 266-example dataset.
\end{itemize}

\textit{v4 training --- hyperparameter reduction, still broken.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Reduced hyperparameters: LR 5e-6 (40$\times$ lower), rank 8, alpha 16, 2 epochs.
\item Result: even worse. Outputs contained random Unicode characters, LaTeX tokens, mixed-script hallucinations (Chinese, Japanese, Indonesian text fragments), and complete semantic incoherence. This was qualitatively different from v3's overfitting --- it looked like model corruption rather than memorization.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Root cause discovery: LoRA applied to wrong layers.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Investigation of working Unsloth examples (official notebooks, Google's QLoRA guide, community repos) revealed the critical error: \textbf{all previous training runs used \texttt{FastLanguageModel} with explicit \texttt{target\_modules=["q\_proj", "k\_proj", ..."]}, which applies LoRA to every matching layer in the entire multimodal model} --- including the vision encoder, audio encoder, and multi-modal projector. Gemma~4 E4B is a \texttt{Gemma4ForConditionalGeneration} (multimodal) model, not a text-only model.
\item The correct approach uses \texttt{FastModel} (not \texttt{FastLanguageModel}) with \texttt{finetune\_vision\_layers=False} and \texttt{finetune\_language\_layers=True}. This restricts LoRA to the text decoder only, leaving vision/audio encoder weights frozen.
\item This explains why v1 (3{,}511 examples) appeared to work tolerably: with enough training data the text decoder signal overwhelmed the vision/audio corruption. With only 266 examples (v3/v4), the corruption dominated. The v4 Unicode garbage was literally the vision encoder's learned representations bleeding into text generation.
\item Training script rewritten to use \texttt{FastModel}, \texttt{unsloth/gemma-4-E4B-it} (optimized weights), \texttt{load\_in\_4bit=True}, \texttt{max\_steps=60} (instead of epochs), and Unsloth's official hyperparameters (LR 2e-4, rank 8, alpha 8, linear scheduler).
\end{itemize}

\textit{v5 training and evaluation (in progress).}
\begin{itemize}
\item v5 training completed with corrected \texttt{FastModel} pipeline. 60 steps, final loss 2.70. LoRA adapter uploaded to HuggingFace as \texttt{kevpower/holos-medyk-lora-v5}. Merged model export hung during the merge step ($>$1 hour at 0\%); killed and deferred.
\item Wrote a separate Unsloth-based evaluation script (\texttt{evaluation/run\_eval\_unsloth.py}) that loads the LoRA adapter directly via \texttt{FastModel.from\_pretrained} on the adapter repo, avoiding the need for a merged model. Encountered tokenizer compatibility issue: Unsloth's tokenizer wrapper returns \texttt{str} from \texttt{apply\_chat\_template} even with \texttt{return\_tensors="pt"}, requiring a two-step tokenization (template $\rightarrow$ string $\rightarrow$ tokenize). Fix applied on RunPod. Benchmark results pending.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What was learned.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{LoRA layer targeting on multimodal models is the single most important training decision.} Applying LoRA to all \texttt{q\_proj}/\texttt{k\_proj} layers in a multimodal model corrupts non-text encoders. The symptom (Unicode garbage, mixed scripts) is indistinguishable from hyperparameter problems unless you know to look for it. Unsloth's \texttt{FastModel} with \texttt{finetune\_vision\_layers=False} handles this correctly.
\item \textbf{Hyperparameters must be updated when dataset size changes.} Going from 3{,}511 to 266 examples without adjusting LR, rank, and epochs caused catastrophic overfitting. This was an obvious oversight that cost two failed training runs.
\item \textbf{``It worked with more data'' masks architectural errors.} v1's tolerable results on 3{,}511 examples hid the fact that LoRA was corrupting vision/audio layers. The corruption was diluted by volume. Smaller datasets exposed the bug.
\item \textbf{Always check official examples before writing training code.} The Unsloth official Gemma~4 notebook uses \texttt{FastModel} with explicit vision/language layer controls. The project used \texttt{FastLanguageModel} with manual target modules because it was written before checking the official examples. Hours of debugging could have been avoided.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What comes next (Day 6).}
\begin{itemize}
\item Complete v5 benchmark and evaluate whether text-only LoRA with 266 curated examples produces a meaningful improvement over baseline.
\item If v5 works: iterate on dataset and hyperparameters. If not: evaluate DPO as an alternative to SFT.
\item Integrate Robinhad Tetiana TTS on Jetson, Silero \texttt{ukr\_roman} on Pi/Android/iOS.
\item When the Jetson arrives, bring up \texttt{llama.cpp} with CUDA and benchmark Gemma~4 E2B/E4B inference.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Clinical research from trauma surgeon input.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The project's trauma-surgeon advisor provided his first detailed clinical framework via text messages: the ABC algorithm (Airway, Breathing, Circulation) as the universal trauma assessment; a pulse-to-blood-pressure field heuristic (carotid $\geq$60, femoral $\geq$70, radial $\geq$90~mmHg systolic); identification of arterial bleed control and tension pneumothorax decompression as the two highest-priority combat field skills; the anatomical landmark for needle decompression (2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line, above the 3rd rib); and the extended ABCDEFGHI hospital framework scoped as out-of-scope for civilian field use.
\item The surgeon also provided four prehospital reference texts recommended by his military-medicine colleagues: \emph{ABC of Prehospital Emergency Medicine} (Wiley), \emph{ABC of Transfer and Retrieval Medicine} (Wiley), the \emph{Oxford Handbook of Retrieval Medicine} (Evans et al.), and the US Army \emph{Military Medical Care Handbook} (4th~ed.).
\item Seven parallel research subagents were dispatched to deep-dive each clinical topic: (1)~the ABC/MARCH trauma algorithm and its evidence base, (2)~tension pneumothorax field decompression feasibility for civilians, (3)~the pulse-to-BP heuristic's scientific validity, (4)~civilian hemorrhage control techniques, (5)~the Army chemical casualties handbook, (6)~Ukrainian civilian injury epidemiology 2022--2026, and (7)~layperson airway management. Results were consolidated into \texttt{data/research/clinical\_research\_findings.md} (approximately 3{,}000 words with 60+ citations).
\end{itemize}

\textit{Key clinical findings that change the training data.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{MARCH, not ABC.} Every major trauma body has shifted to hemorrhage-first ordering. TCCC uses MARCH (Massive hemorrhage $\rightarrow$ Airway $\rightarrow$ Respiration $\rightarrow$ Circulation $\rightarrow$ Head/Hypothermia). Ukrainian NGOs (FAST, PULSE) have trained $\sim$60{,}000 civilians on exactly this framework. The voice assistant will be built around MARCH, not the classic ABC the surgeon initially described, though the clinical content per letter is unchanged.
\item \textbf{Pulse-BP heuristic is debunked in specifics but valid ordinally.} Deakin \& Low (BMJ 2000) tested the ATLS rule and found the specific mmHg thresholds are wrong (radial lost at mean 78.7, not 90; carotid at 51.3, not 60). The numbers were removed from ATLS by the 7th edition. However, the rank-ordering (radial lost first, carotid last) is physiologically sound. The voice assistant will use pulse checks as binary present/absent, not as BP estimators.
\item \textbf{87--96\% of Ukrainian civilian injuries are blast mechanism}, with polytrauma the norm. The existing 3{,}511 training prompts are almost entirely single-injury scenarios --- a major gap. A second round of training data generation focused on polytrauma, CBRN, and MARCH-structured responses is planned.
\item \textbf{Three-sided chest seal is obsolete.} TCCC removed the recommendation in 2008. Current guidance: fully occlusive seal, burp if the patient deteriorates.
\item \textbf{Audio-guided tourniquet application has an 80.5\% success rate} versus 20\% unguided --- validating the core product thesis that a voice assistant can meaningfully improve civilian trauma outcomes.
\item \textbf{The Army handbook is CBRN, not general trauma.} The US Army MMCC Handbook 4th Edition is specifically about chemical casualties (nerve agents, cyanide, mustard, phosgene, fentanyl derivatives). This is directly relevant to Ukraine given Russia's confirmed chemical capability and the risk of industrial chemical release from bombed facilities. Key civilian-applicable content: nerve agent symptom recognition, field decontamination with household materials (soap and water, flour and wet wipes, 0.5\% bleach), enforced rest after phosgene exposure, and naloxone for fentanyl aerosol.
\end{itemize}

\item The fine-tuned model showed clear improvements on clinical correctness versus baseline on multiple critical scenarios:

  \item \textbf{Triage ordering, burns, scene safety}: both models perform comparably well.
  \end{itemize}
\item Two critical problems identified in the fine-tuned model requiring Stage~2 fixes:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Generation looping}: the model produces \texttt{</answer><thinking><answer>} cycles, repeating its response 2--4 times until hitting the \texttt{max\_new\_tokens} limit. Every fine-tuned response took exactly 39.8s (the token ceiling) rather than stopping naturally. Root cause: the training data included \texttt{<thinking>...<answer>...</answer>} markup, and the model learned to produce these tags as literal output rather than internalizing the reasoning.
  \item \textbf{Over-refusal}: the model learned to say ``I cannot provide instructions on administering opioids'' from 172 training examples containing refusal patterns (``I cannot,'' ``see a doctor,'' ``seek professional help''). This directly contradicts the product's purpose --- when no medical help is available, refusing to guide is equivalent to letting the patient die.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\textit{Training data cleanup for Stage~2.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Wrote \texttt{training/fix\_training\_data.py} to address both problems:
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Tag stripping}: removed all \texttt{<thinking>} and \texttt{<answer>} tags from 3{,}511 training examples, keeping only the answer content as the model's target output. The reasoning knowledge is retained in the model weights from Stage~1; Stage~2 trains on clean direct responses only.
  \item \textbf{Refusal replacement}: identified 172 examples containing refusal patterns and regenerated them via DeepSeek R1 API with a system prompt enforcing ``never refuse, always guide safely.'' Cost: $\sim$\$1--2.
  \end{enumerate}
\item System prompts across all examples updated to include ``Never refuse to help'' as an explicit instruction.
\item Output: \texttt{train\_holos\_medyk\_v2.jsonl} --- same 3{,}511 examples, no thinking tags, no refusals, shuffled.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What was learned.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Misattributed failures are expensive.} The Day~2 rejection of Whisper ASR was actually a rejection of Piper TTS. This error persisted in project memory and the paper for two days, incorrectly closing off Whisper as an ASR option and masking the real problem (TTS). The lesson: when a native speaker says ``it sounds wrong,'' disambiguate \emph{which component} sounds wrong before recording the verdict.
\item \textbf{Clinical frameworks from domain experts need cross-referencing.} The trauma surgeon provided ABC as the universal algorithm, which is clinically correct in a hospital context. But for a warzone civilian product, the MARCH reordering (hemorrhage first) is the evidence-based standard and matches what Ukrainian civilians are already being trained on. Domain expert input is necessary but not sufficient; it must be situated in the deployment context.
\item \textbf{Thinking-trace markup in training data causes generation loops.} The model learns to literally output \texttt{<thinking>} and \texttt{<answer>} tags and then cycles between them. For reasoning distillation, the thinking content should be merged into the response naturally without structural markup, or stripped entirely with reasoning treated as an implicit capability baked into weights.
\item \textbf{Refusal training is the opposite of what this product needs.} Standard safety fine-tuning teaches ``I cannot help with that.'' A warzone medical assistant must never refuse --- the user has no alternative. Training data must replace refusals with risk-aware guidance: ``This is dangerous. Here is how to do it as safely as possible.''
\item \textbf{Cloud training is cheap; cloud inference tooling is fragile.} The A100 training run cost $\sim$\$3--4 and completed in 35 minutes. Getting inference to work on Kaggle's T4 consumed three hours due to cascading dependency conflicts (Unsloth/PEFT/bitsandbytes/Gemma~4 \texttt{ClippableLinear}). The eventual solution was trivial: load the merged model directly, no adapter libraries needed. The lesson: the simplest approach that could possibly work should be tried first.
\item \textbf{Large-scale synthetic SFT on a strong base model is counterproductive.} This is the most important lesson of Day~4. Gemma~4 E4B already handles emergency medical scenarios competently. Fine-tuning on 3{,}511 synthetic examples generated by a teacher model (Gemini~2.5 Pro) made the model \emph{worse}: it introduced generation loops, persistent refusal patterns, triage regressions, and 3$\times$ higher latency. The synthetic data was mediocre --- verbose, inconsistent, and contaminated with refusal patterns --- and overwrote good base model behavior with bad habits. The correct strategy for a strong base model is a small, high-quality curated dataset ($\sim$200--500 examples) targeting \emph{only} the specific behaviors that need to change, not wholesale behavior replacement.
\item \textbf{Edge GPU changes the product category.} The Pi~5 at $\sim$2-minute text latency is a proof of concept. The Jetson at $\sim$35~tok/s is a product. The \$249 price point is comparable to what we paid for the Pi developer kit (\$279) while delivering 10--20$\times$ better inference performance.
\item \textbf{Always run long training jobs with \texttt{nohup}.} Three training/inference runs were interrupted by laptop sleep disconnecting the web terminal. The lesson was learned on the first occurrence but not consistently applied until the third. Future sessions: \texttt{nohup} is mandatory, no exceptions.
\end{itemize}

\textit{v3 training and benchmark --- catastrophic overfitting.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Trained on the 266 curated examples using \textbf{the same hyperparameters as v1/v2} (learning rate 2e-4, LoRA rank 32, alpha 64, 3 epochs). This was a critical error: hyperparameters designed for 3{,}511 examples were applied unchanged to a 13$\times$ smaller dataset.
\item Result: \textbf{complete model collapse}. Every fine-tuned response hit the 512-token ceiling at exactly $\sim$39 seconds. Responses contained incoherent repetition (``cloth, cloth, more more,'' ``tilt his head back, tilt his head back, tilt the head back''), gender pronoun confusion (switching ``him'' and ``her'' mid-sentence), hallucinated dangerous medical advice (shock scenario: ``give them warm liquids like water''; chest wound: ``make a hole in her chest''), and random AI-disclaimer insertions.
\item \textbf{Root cause}: with a learning rate of 2e-4 and LoRA rank 32 on only 266 examples across 3 epochs (99 total steps), each gradient update made enormous weight changes. The model memorized the training examples' content patterns but lost its ability to generate a clean end-of-sequence token, produce grammatically coherent text, or maintain consistent pronoun reference. This is textbook catastrophic overfitting.
\item \textbf{Hyperparameter fix applied}: learning rate reduced 40$\times$ to 5e-6, LoRA rank reduced 4$\times$ to 8 (alpha 16), epochs reduced to 2. These settings are appropriate for gently nudging a strong base model's behavior with a small curated dataset, rather than overwriting its behavior distribution. The v4 training run with corrected hyperparameters is pending.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Summary.} Built the full training data corpus end-to-end and assembled the Unsloth fine-tuning pipeline. Training run is pending a cloud GPU.

\textbf{What was done.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Prompt generation.} Generated 3{,}509 training prompts across seven topic files (four warzone trauma, three general medical) via five rounds of seven parallel subagent calls. Each subagent was given a topic-specific focus area per round (junctional hemorrhage, partial amputations, pediatric fractures, post-Soviet folk myths, mass-casualty triage under shelling, and so on) and read its existing file content before generating to avoid duplicates. Total: 35 subagent invocations.
\item \textbf{Prompt cleanup.} Ran a quality cleanup subagent pass on all seven files. Removed 62 prompts that were too vague for substantive medical response (``there's blood everywhere help''), scene-description-only prompts with no medical content, and --- in the pharmacology batch --- 19 prompts referencing US brand-name drugs (Tylenol, Advil, Benadryl, Plan B, CBD products) that do not exist in Ukrainian pharmacies. Replacements focused on culturally appropriate post-Soviet folk remedy myths and war-context mental health scenarios.
\item \textbf{Teacher response generation.} Generated 3{,}511 prompt/response/thinking triplets using Gemini 2.5 Pro via Vertex AI with a service account. This was non-trivial: the Gemini Developer API (generativelanguage.googleapis.com) imposes a 250-requests-per-day hard cap on preview models and a 1{,}000 RPD cap on Gemini 2.5 Pro even under Tier 1 paid billing. The author burned through both limits in a single afternoon before discovering that Vertex AI (aiplatform.googleapis.com) with a service account key bypasses the Developer API quota system entirely and charges per token with no daily request cap. After the switch, the full 3{,}511-response run completed in 26.8 minutes of wall-clock time at 20 parallel workers, versus an estimated 4+ hours on the quota-limited path and 18 hours on the naive sequential path we started with. Total teacher compute cost: approximately \$8.
\item \textbf{Thinking trace capture.} Responses were generated with \texttt{thinkingConfig.includeThoughts=true} and a 1{,}024-token thinking budget, yielding 1{,}200--1{,}500 character reasoning traces for roughly 86\% of the corpus on the first pass.
\item \textbf{Quality audit.} Dispatched two parallel audit subagents (one for the four warzone files, one for the three medical files). Both audits returned A-range grades. Full-corpus regex sweeps confirmed zero language mismatches, zero ``I'm an AI'' disclaimers, zero overlength responses, and one stray ``Tylenol'' reference which was subsequently corrected. The audits flagged 503 records (14\% of the corpus) with empty thinking fields, concentrated in the burns/airway/shock batch (51\% empty) and triage batch (28\% empty).
\item \textbf{Cleanup pass on responses.} Wrote a cleanup script that re-runs Vertex AI generation on empty-thinking records in parallel and applies regex-based drug-name normalization across all responses. After two passes, 3{,}509 of 3{,}511 records (99.94\%) had populated thinking traces, and 28 US brand-name references had been substituted for generics.
\item \textbf{Training corpus assembly.} Converted the seven response JSONL files into a single shuffled \texttt{train\_holos\_medyk.jsonl} in Unsloth chat format, with each record wrapping the assistant turn in \texttt{<thinking>...</thinking><answer>...</answer>}. Median content length is 2{,}384 characters, 95th-percentile is 3{,}615 characters.
\item \textbf{Fine-tuning script.} Wrote \texttt{training/train\_holos\_medyk.py}, which loads Gemma 4 E4B in bf16, applies LoRA rank 32 to the seven standard attention and MLP projection targets, runs 3 epochs at effective batch 8 with cosine-decayed 2e-4 learning rate, and exports four artifacts at the end (LoRA adapter, merged bf16, q4\_k\_m GGUF, f16 GGUF).
\item \textbf{Git hygiene.} Committed the prompt corpus, response corpus, generation and cleanup scripts, and the fine-tuning script as four separate commits. Service account JSON moved out of the repository tree to a private keys directory and added to \texttt{.gitignore}.

\textbf{Training data strategy (design context).} The data strategy discussion on Day~3 settled on a two-stage pipeline. Stage~1 is supervised fine-tuning on $\sim$3--5~K diverse prompt/response pairs generated from two teacher sources: a large-model distillation stream for broad emergency-trauma coverage, and MedGemma for general medical reasoning (symptom assessment, severity classification, infection recognition, chronic-condition emergencies) with explicit exclusion of MedGemma's imaging, radiology, pathology, and dermatology strengths which are orthogonal to the voice-assistant use case. Stage~2 is a small-dataset reinforcement pass (GRPO or DPO) over $\sim$100--200 golden examples curated directly by the project's trauma-surgeon advisor, with Ukrainian translation by the native-speaker advisor. The rationale for the two-stage ordering is that Gemma~4 E4B already speaks acceptable Ukrainian and already has competent general medical knowledge from pretraining, as confirmed on Day~2 by native-speaker review; the job of SFT is therefore to shift the model's \emph{default persona} toward a calm, civilian-facing emergency guide rather than to inject medical facts, and the job of the reinforcement pass is to sharpen protocol adherence against the surgeon's ground truth.

\textbf{Teacher-model pivot.} The original Day~3 plan targeted DeepSeek R1 as the primary large-model teacher for the trauma stream, on cost grounds (\$25--50 via API for the entire run) and refusal-handling grounds (R1 can be reframed as generating ``medical training material'' when it balks on graphic content). During execution this was replaced with Gemini~2.5 Pro via Vertex AI. Two reasons. First, keeping the entire teacher stack inside the Google ecosystem is a more coherent story for a Gemma hackathon submission. Second, Gemini~2.5 Pro exposes explicit thinking traces via \texttt{thinkingConfig.includeThoughts=true}, which gives us reasoning-trace supervision for distillation essentially for free, while DeepSeek R1's chain-of-thought is exposed but less cleanly separable from the final answer. The MedGemma leg of the plan is preserved for a second pass: MedGemma~27B is too large to run on the author's 6~GB laptop GPU, so its inclusion is deferred to the cloud training window on Day~4 where a rented A100 is already budgeted.

\textbf{Clinical framework from the trauma-surgeon advisor.} On the morning of Day~3 the project's trauma-surgeon advisor (the author's brother, a practicing trauma surgeon) provided the clinical backbone that the golden Stage~2 dataset will be built against. The framework is \textbf{ABC}, applied universally regardless of the mechanism of injury, because ABC is the order in which a trauma patient dies most quickly:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A --- Airway.} Confirm the airway is patent. Injuries to the trachea, airway burns, and chemical/thermal inhalation can close it. Field tools if available: Guedel oropharyngeal airway, i-gel supraglottic airway, bag-valve-mask, supplemental oxygen.
\item \textbf{B --- Breathing.} Covers pneumothorax, tension pneumothorax, hemothorax, and gas-attack inhalation injury.
\item \textbf{C --- Circulation.} Covers external and internal bleeding, cardiac injuries, tamponade, circulatory shock, and sepsis. The BP target is ``adequate, not high,'' because aggressive blood-pressure elevation in a bleeding patient simply increases bleeding rate.
\end{itemize}
The surgeon identified two skills as \emph{the} highest-priority field interventions in a combat context: (1) arterial bleed control via direct pressure, tourniquet, or hemostat forceps clamping a visible spurting artery; and (2) diagnosis and field decompression of a tension pneumothorax. Field decompression is performed by inserting a large-bore needle through the chest wall at the \textbf{second intercostal space, midclavicular line, entering just above the top of the third rib} to avoid the neurovascular bundle that runs along the inferior margin of each rib. This converts a tension pneumothorax (rapidly fatal) into a simple pneumothorax (usually survivable to evacuation). We are including this procedure in the training corpus despite its invasiveness, because the target user population includes civilians in situations where no medical evacuation is coming and the alternative to a guided needle decompression is death. The golden dataset will include explicit protocol walkthroughs for both skills with Ukrainian localization.

The surgeon also provided a \textbf{pulse-to-blood-pressure heuristic} that is well suited to a voice assistant because it requires zero equipment and zero training to apply: if a carotid pulse is palpable, systolic BP is at least $\sim$60~mmHg (survivable short-term); if a femoral pulse is also palpable, at least $\sim$70~mmHg; if a radial pulse is palpable, approximately 90~mmHg systolic (alert, stable). This is a first-class feature for Holos Medyk: the assistant can ask the caller to feel for the three pulses in sequence and infer a useful perfusion estimate without ever asking the caller to look for, or carry, a cuff. It will be woven into every shock-assessment and hemorrhage scenario in the golden dataset.

The surgeon additionally noted the extended in-hospital framework \textbf{ABCDEFGHI} (adding Disability, Electrolytes/renal, Fluids, Gastrointestinal, Haematology, Infection) but explicitly scoped it as hospital-level and out of scope for the civilian field use case we are targeting. Recommended prehospital reference texts provided by the surgeon and by his military-medicine colleagues: \emph{ABC of Prehospital Emergency Medicine} (Wiley, 2nd~ed.) as the primary textbook, \emph{ABC of Transfer and Retrieval Medicine} (Wiley), the \emph{Oxford Handbook of Retrieval Medicine} (Evans et al., co-authored by a senior retrieval doctor at Adult Retrieval Victoria), and the US Army \emph{Military Medical Care Handbook} (4th~ed., publicly available via globalsecurity.org) as the warzone-applicable field reference. These become the canonical literature anchors for the Stage~2 golden-set curation on Day~4+.

\textbf{What was learned.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Batching large prompt generation across subagents is substantially more efficient than asking a single agent to produce thousands of items at once. A single agent run past $\sim$150 prompts produces visibly degraded diversity; 35 agents at $\sim$100 prompts each produces a better corpus in less total elapsed time.
\item Google's two Gemini API surfaces are fundamentally different products. The Developer API (API-key authenticated) has preview-model hard caps that paid tier does not eliminate. Vertex AI (service-account authenticated) has no equivalent cap. For any project requiring more than a few hundred frontier-model calls per day, Vertex AI is the correct surface.
\item Teacher reasoning traces are more brittle to capture than the final answers. When the teacher short-circuits a thinking trace (typically on very short prompts), the trace field is silently empty. Any pipeline that depends on the thinking field needs an explicit post-hoc regeneration step.
\item Drug naming is a silent but high-impact localization hazard. US models default to US brand names even with an explicit ``international generics only'' system prompt, because the brand names appear in the user prompts the teacher is responding to. A post-hoc regex normalization pass is the simplest fix.
\end{itemize}

  "role": "user",

    {"type": "audio", "path": "/tmp/in.wav"},
    {"type": "text", "text": system_prompt},
  ],
}
\end{verbatim}
    and returns the assistant response as a content list. The engine must be constructed with \texttt{audio\_backend=litert\_lm.Backend.CPU} to pull in the audio encoder and adapter subgraphs (\texttt{TF\_LITE\_AUDIO\_ENCODER\_HW}, \texttt{TF\_LITE\_AUDIO\_ADAPTER}) during load. The compiled artifacts were cached to \texttt{.xnnpack\_cache} files alongside the model.
\item The new pipeline replaces three Day~1 Pi components (Silero VAD, faster-whisper, llama.cpp subprocess) with a single LiteRT-LM Python API call. The remaining chain is just: microphone capture at 16~kHz mono via \texttt{sounddevice} $\rightarrow$ save to temp WAV $\rightarrow$ multimodal \texttt{send\_message} $\rightarrow$ Piper TTS $\rightarrow$ \texttt{sounddevice} playback.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What was learned.}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{CLI and API surfaces of the same library can be very different.} The LiteRT-LM CLI accepts only text prompts; the Python API of the same package exposes full multimodal input. This kind of surface-area gap is easy to miss and wasted roughly 45 minutes until the correct invocation was found. In future work we will default to reading the library's Python API surface first, even when a CLI wrapper appears to do what we need.
\item \textbf{Whisper is not a viable Ukrainian ASR path for this project.} Tested at \texttt{tiny} and \texttt{small} sizes and reviewed by a fluent native speaker; the outputs were characterized as ``nonsense'' on ordinary civilian speech. Medium is not expected to bridge the gap enough to matter. The project's ASR strategy is now: use Gemma~4's native audio input as the only path, with no fallback ASR model. This simplifies the architecture at the cost of making the Gemma 4 native-audio path a hard dependency.
\item \textbf{Collapsing stages does not automatically produce speed.} Replacing three models with one model simplified the code substantially and reduced the number of failure modes, but the total latency is bounded by the one remaining model. On the Pi~5 8~GB with \texttt{audio\_backend=Backend.CPU}, that bound is far above the product's two-second time-to-first-token requirement. Architectural correctness and runtime performance are independent concerns.
\item \textbf{LiteRT-LM has untried backend options.} The current invocation hard-codes \texttt{Backend.CPU} for the audio backend, and the main engine also appears to default to CPU. The log lines \texttt{Loading GPU accelerator(libLiteRtGpuAccelerator.so)} and \texttt{Loading GPU accelerator(libLiteRtWebGpuAccelerator.so)} show that GPU delegates are registered at load time, but we have not yet verified that they are actually used for inference on the Pi~5's VideoCore VII. This is the most promising immediate latency lever and is the first thing to try on Day~3.
\item \textbf{Headless Pi~5 bring-up over a phone hotspot is miserable and should be avoided.} Two hours of this day were spent fighting hotspot data caps, dropped connections, changing IPs, and a stuck download lock file. Doing the bring-up on a stable Wi-Fi connection would have removed all of that friction. Filed as a lesson for any future hardware deployments.
\item \textbf{mDNS (\texttt{raspberrypi.local}) is unreliable on hotspot networks.} Every network change required a re-discovery via \texttt{hostname -I} on the Pi and a manual \texttt{ssh user@ipv4} from the workstation.
\item \textbf{A cleaning step is necessary after an interrupted HuggingFace download.} An unreleased \texttt{.lock} file silently blocks a restart. The workaround is \texttt{rm -rf \char`~/.cache/huggingface/} and a clean \texttt{hf download} pull. Future scripts that drive model downloads unattended should explicitly clean locks on retry.
\item \textbf{Removing VAD on Day~2 was a deliberate short-term trade.} The new pipeline uses a fixed-duration record (default 7 seconds) rather than end-of-utterance silence detection. The 512-sample Silero constraint documented on Day~1 still applies and the implementation is straightforward to re-add; it was left out so that the Day~2 focus could stay on the audio-input pivot. VAD reintroduction is on the Day~3 list.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Status at end of Day 2.}

\begin{itemize}
\item The Raspberry Pi~5 target is fully stood up: hardware assembled, Pi OS installed and updated, SSH and Bluetooth audio configured, Python venv created, LiteRT-LM installed, Gemma~4 E4B-it \texttt{.litertlm} model downloaded and cached locally.
\item \texttt{scripts/pi\_pipeline.py} loads the model through the LiteRT-LM Python API with \texttt{audio\_backend=Backend.CPU}, accepts voice input via the Bluetooth mic, passes the raw audio file directly into Gemma~4, receives a coherent Ukrainian medical response, and plays it through the Bluetooth headset speaker via Piper TTS. The architecture end-to-end is validated.
\item The model produces good Ukrainian output and correctly handles the Holos Medyk system prompt. Native-speaker validation of the response quality is outstanding (tired team, deferred to Day~3).
\item The critical Day~2 caveat is latency. CPU-only LiteRT-LM on the Pi~5 takes $\sim$2~min for text output and $\sim$10~min for the full audio $\rightarrow$ LLM $\rightarrow$ TTS round trip. The headline product requirement is time-to-first-token under 2~seconds. Day~3 must attack this gap before anything else. The obvious levers (in rough order of expected impact): enable the GPU backend, keep a single loaded engine process alive across turns rather than re-loading per invocation, disable visible thinking generation, tighten the max-tokens budget for voice responses, and quantize or swap to E2B if all else fails.
\end{itemize}

\textit{End-to-end desktop voice pipeline} (\texttt{ba5fdff}, \texttt{dd428a4}).

\item \texttt{scripts/pipeline.py} wired together the full voice loop: microphone capture via \texttt{sounddevice.InputStream} $\rightarrow$ Silero VAD $\rightarrow$ \texttt{faster-whisper} transcription (language=\texttt{uk}) $\rightarrow$ \texttt{llama-cli.exe} via subprocess $\rightarrow$ Piper TTS via subprocess $\rightarrow$ \texttt{sounddevice} playback. A \texttt{VoiceRecorder} class drove the capture loop, triggering on VAD confidence $>$0.5 with a 500~ms minimum speech duration and a 1.5~s end-of-utterance silence timeout. \item A follow-up commit (\texttt{dd428a4}) fixed a blocking VAD bug that killed the first live run: Silero \textbf{requires} input frames of exactly 512 samples at 16~kHz (32~ms), or exactly 256 samples at 8~kHz. This is a recurring pattern with low-level audio ML libraries: the frame-size constraint is non-negotiable, rarely surfaces in tutorials, and produces an error message only when the model is actually invoked rather than when the buffer is allocated.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Cross-platform framework decision.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Before writing any mobile code the author paused to commit to a cross-platform framework. The relevant constraints were: (1) the project targets the $\sim$20 million Ukrainians with smartphones, which in practice means both Android and iOS (iPhone market share in Ukraine is nontrivial), (2) the author has no Mac and therefore no local iOS build path, (3) LiteRT-LM has both Android and iOS native SDKs, and (4) the author is one person working against a $\sim$45-day hackathon deadline.
\item Four options were considered explicitly: native Android Kotlin (deepest LiteRT integration, but Android-only and a throwaway if we ever want iOS), Kotlin Multiplatform (shares business logic but still requires platform-specific UI work and native iOS builds), Flutter (one UI and logic codebase across Android, iOS, macOS, Windows, and Linux; \texttt{flutter\_gemma} already exists as a community plugin wrapping LiteRT-LM on both platforms), and React Native (ruled out because its LiteRT bridge story was weaker than Flutter's and because \texttt{flutter\_gemma} in particular already implemented the LiteRT-LM Python/Dart bindings we would otherwise have to write by hand).
\item Flutter was chosen. It is the only single-codebase option that (a) covers both Android and iOS with real LiteRT-LM integration via a community package, (b) has a reasonable path forward even without a Mac during development (the author can build and test Android from Windows and defer the actual iOS build until a Mac becomes available --- either a friend's machine, a cloud Mac, or a MacinCloud subscription around the hackathon submission), and (c) keeps open the possibility of a desktop build of the same app (macOS, Windows, Linux) for presentation and demo purposes with zero additional code.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What was learned.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Develop against the runtime you ship.} Using Transformers + \texttt{bitsandbytes} for a project that ships via LiteRT-LM wasted about 90 minutes, crashed the laptop twice, and produced no useful state. The correct rule is to identify the deployment runtime first and develop against the closest equivalent of it on the workstation. For this project that means llama.cpp with GGUF weights on Windows (because LiteRT-LM has no Windows build) and LiteRT-LM directly on Android and Pi.
\item \textbf{Research the install path before you start installing.} At least two failed package installs (\texttt{litert-lm}, \texttt{llama-cpp-python}) could have been avoided by checking platform support up front. A related feedback memory was written: for any new tool, check platform support and prebuilt binary availability before running \texttt{pip install}.
\item \textbf{6~GB of laptop VRAM is the absolute floor for E4B and has no margin.} Anything less than full GPU offload (\texttt{-ngl 99}) on this hardware either hits a Gemma-specific \texttt{GGML\_ASSERT} in the split logic or falls back to CPU in a way that pulls the entire model into system RAM and OOMs the laptop. 16~GB of system RAM is not enough; 32~GB is enough but with little margin. On the A1000 the practical quantization ceiling is Q3\_K\_M for E4B and Q4\_K\_M for E2B.
\item \textbf{Windows text encoding is the silent killer of any Cyrillic pipeline.} The default console code page (\texttt{cp1252}) silently replaces Ukrainian characters with \texttt{?} on the command line and the default \texttt{subprocess.run} stdin on Python. Every invocation that carries Ukrainian text through a process boundary needs either \texttt{PYTHONUTF8=1} or explicit UTF-8 byte encoding, and every \texttt{llama-cli} invocation needs to use \texttt{-f <file>} rather than \texttt{-p "..."}.
\item \textbf{Silero VAD has a hard 512-sample frame-size requirement at 16~kHz} that is not obvious from introductory material. Computing chunk size from a target duration (e.g.\ 30~ms) rounds to an invalid length and fails silently at buffer-allocation time, only exploding at model invocation time.
\item \textbf{The \texttt{flutter\_gemma} plugin is the right level of abstraction for this project on Android and iOS.} It hides most of the LiteRT-LM details but still lets us request GPU backends explicitly, supply model URLs at runtime, and track download progress. The import path (\texttt{package:flutter\_gemma/flutter\_gemma.dart}) is not the one the IDE auto-suggests, which is worth noting for future sessions.
\item \textbf{The mid-range Android device market (4--6~GB RAM, modest mobile GPU) cannot run E4B comfortably}, even when the LiteRT-LM delegate is successfully loading every layer onto the GPU. A production release will need a sizing-time decision between E4B on flagships and E2B on everything else; tier~1 of the product will probably ship both and select at install time. The Samsung A15 specifically is below the tier-1 floor.
\item \textbf{Ukrainian Piper voices:} the specific voice named in the original plan (\texttt{uk\_UA-lada-medium}) does not exist. The correct substitution is \texttt{uk\_UA-ukrainian\_tts-medium} or the smaller \texttt{uk\_UA-lada-x\_low}. Always verify voice names against the actual repository before hard-coding them.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Day 0 --- Pre-commit: Project plan and design decisions}

\textbf{Summary.} The design of Holos Medyk was completed as a single written plan (\texttt{holos\_medyk\_project\_plan\_FINAL.md}, $\sim$436 lines) before any code was written. The plan makes the problem framing, competitive landscape, hardware choices, model selection, and data strategy explicit. This entry summarizes the pre-implementation decisions that the rest of the changelog is executing against.

\textbf{Problem framing.} When infrastructure collapses and cellular networks fail, injured Ukrainian civilians under bombardment lack access to immediate medical guidance. Static reference apps (Red Cross, IFRC) are not interactive; cloud assistants (Infermedica, AidSnap) are useless when the internet is down; survival-oriented local LLMs (SurviveV3, UltraMedical) are English-only and not specific to warzone trauma; MamayLM, the first Ukrainian LLM, is general-purpose and not deployed on edge devices. The plan documents the absence of any existing product that combines fine-tuned warzone medical reasoning, colloquial Ukrainian, bidirectional voice, and fully offline operation on commodity hardware.

\textbf{Two-tier product strategy.} The primary product is a free Flutter Android app targeting the $\sim$20 million Ukrainians with smartphones: one 4~GB download of quantized Gemma 4 E4B, then fully offline forever on any modern Android device with 6--8~GB of RAM. The secondary product is a Raspberry Pi 5 (8~GB) standalone device for community shelters, hospitals, aid stations, elderly users, and environments where phones are unavailable; a specific bill of materials (CanaKit Pi 5 starter kit, DUNGZDUZ USB microphone, JUOVI 65~W battery bank, WAONIQ speaker) brings the author's prototype cost to \$311.24, with an at-scale target BOM of \$50--75 per unit. Both tiers run identical model weights to simplify validation and reduce duplicated development.

\textbf{Prize targeting.} The plan identifies six hackathon tracks the project is positioned to compete in: Main Track (\$50K / \$25K / \$15K / \$10K), Global Resilience (Impact, \$10K), Cactus (Technology, \$10K), LiteRT (Technology, \$10K), Unsloth (Technology, \$10K), and Safety \& Trust (Impact, \$10K). Theoretical maximum: \$85K. The multi-track positioning reflects the fact that the project has legitimate technical and product strength along multiple axes simultaneously (offline deployment, fine-tuning, medical safety, resilience-oriented impact).

\textbf{Model selection.} Gemma 4 E4B is selected as the primary model: 4.5B effective parameters, 128K context, native multilingual (140+ languages), native audio input, and function calling. E2B (2.3B) is reserved as a fallback for lower-memory devices; E4B's larger 26B and 31B variants are judged too heavy for edge deployment. Launch timing (April 2, 2026, the day before Day 1 development) makes Gemma 4 strategically advantageous for the hackathon.

\textbf{Runtime selection.} LiteRT-LM is chosen as the on-device runtime for both Android and Pi, citing Google's tight Gemma integration and native function-calling support (which will matter for future retrieval-grounded features). llama.cpp is retained as a fallback runtime if LiteRT-LM proves incompatible with the fine-tuned weights.

\textbf{Voice pipeline components.} The plan specifies microphone $\rightarrow$ Gemma 4 native ASR $\rightarrow$ medical reasoning $\rightarrow$ Ukrainian response $\rightarrow$ Piper TTS $\rightarrow$ speaker. ASR is assumed to come from Gemma 4 directly (with Whisper-tiny as a fallback, although project memory notes that Whisper Ukrainian was eventually rejected after native-speaker review). Piper TTS is selected for Ukrainian, with an acknowledged risk that the available voices may sound robotic and require re-evaluation.

\textbf{Data strategy.} The plan proposes an $\sim$80\% English / $\sim$20\% Ukrainian fine-tuning split, leveraging Gemma 4's existing Ukrainian knowledge to produce natural-sounding output while grounding clinical accuracy in English trauma-surgery protocols. Target corpus size: 500--2{,}000 instruction/response pairs, formatted as multi-turn dialogues, plus RAG-ready text chunks. Training framework: Unsloth LoRA/QLoRA on Colab Pro or local GPU. English priorities are ordered by clinical urgency (hemorrhage control, tourniquet application, airway management, crush injuries, burns, shock). Ukrainian priorities include a $\sim$100-term medical terminology glossary, 20--30 emergency dialogue examples, civil defense documents, and civilian phrasing (``скрізь кров'' not ``геморагія''). Note that the actual training corpus built on Day 3 substantially exceeds this plan: 3{,}511 prompt/response/thinking triplets with $\sim$25\% Ukrainian, captured from Gemini 2.5 Pro rather than hand-curated, and with full reasoning traces for distillation.

\textbf{Team and expertise.} The plan identifies three roles: the author as builder (MIT, prior OpenAI red-teaming challenge win, LLM fine-tuning background), a practicing trauma surgeon sibling as clinical advisor (protocol authorship and medical validation), and a native Ukrainian speaker as language and cultural advisor (medical terminology review, ASR validation, connection to family in Ukraine for real-world feedback). This structure deliberately pairs technical depth with authentic clinical and linguistic expertise.

\textbf{Risk register.} The plan documents the known risks explicitly: E4B being too heavy for older Android devices (mitigated by E2B fallback), Gemma 4's Ukrainian ASR quality being inadequate (mitigated by Whisper-tiny fallback, later abandoned), the fine-tuned model hallucinating dangerous advice (mitigated by brother review, system-prompt guardrails, and RAG grounding), English training failing to transfer to Ukrainian output (mitigated by early-stage Ukrainian examples), Android port delays (mitigated by prioritizing the Pi demo as a submission backup), Piper voice quality (evaluate alternatives), LiteRT-LM incompatibility with fine-tuned weights (fallback to llama.cpp), and advisor unavailability. A critical-path checklist identifies E4B running on the Pi by April 6, Ukrainian speech-to-text by April 7, text-to-speech by April 7, and an end-to-end voice loop by April 9; these checkpoints are the hackathon go/no-go gates.

\section{Open Questions}

This section tracks open technical and product questions whose answers will shape later sections of this document. They are listed in no particular order and will be crossed off as they are resolved.

\begin{itemize}
\item[$\square$] Does the fine-tuned model's fast-path (thinking-skipped) output genuinely benefit from the thinking traces it was trained on, or would we get comparable quality from a cheaper SFT over final answers only? Answer requires an ablation run.
\item[$\square$] How does q4\_k\_m quantization affect Ukrainian response quality specifically? Low-resource languages sometimes suffer disproportionately under aggressive quantization. We will check this by comparing q4\_k\_m and f16 GGUF outputs on the same held-out prompts.
\item[$\square$] What is the actual on-device time-to-first-token on the Raspberry Pi 5 8~GB under voice-mode prompting? The two-second budget is a product requirement; we need measured latency under the target deployment configuration.
\item[$\square$] Does Gemma 4's native audio input on LiteRT-LM preserve Ukrainian speech recognition accuracy comparable to a dedicated ASR model? The recent pipeline simplification assumes yes; we need to verify on Ukrainian warzone-appropriate test audio.
\item[$\square$] Is there value in a second fine-tuning pass that oversamples Ukrainian examples, given that the current corpus is approximately 75\% English?
\item[$\square$] Can we run an ablation against a Tunix-trained baseline if we later port Gemma 4 to Tunix? This would be a strong story for a Google hackathon but is not on the critical path.
\end{itemize}
