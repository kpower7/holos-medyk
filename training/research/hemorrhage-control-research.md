## 1. Why Hemorrhage First: The MARCH Algorithm

**MARCH** = **M**assive hemorrhage, **A**irway, **R**espiration, **C**irculation, **H**ypothermia

Massive hemorrhage is addressed FIRST -- before airway, before everything -- because:

- An arterial bleed can cause death in **2-5 minutes** (aorta: 30-60 seconds)
- A severed femoral artery can pump out a fatal blood volume in **3-5 minutes**
- Loss of 1/3 blood volume is life-threatening; loss of 1/2 to 2/3 is usually fatal
- ~60,000 Americans die annually from hemorrhage; 30-40% of trauma deaths are from blood loss
- Hemorrhage is the **#1 cause of preventable death** in trauma (military and civilian)

**The core civilian message:** *"Stop the bleeding first. Nothing else matters if they bleed to death in 3 minutes."*

### The Four Ds (TCCC Framework for Hemorrhage)
1. **Detect** -- Find the source of bleeding
2. **Direct pressure** -- Immediate hands-on pressure
3. **Devices** -- Tourniquet, hemostatic gauze, pressure bandage
4. **Don't dilute** -- Avoid thinning blood / disrupting clots (advanced, less relevant for civilian first response)

## 2. Direct Pressure

### Proper Technique

1. **Expose the wound** -- Remove or cut away clothing to see where bleeding is coming from
2. **Place hands directly on the wound** -- Use both hands, one on top of the other
3. **Lean body weight into the wound** -- Arms straight, shoulders over hands. For large vessel bleeding, **extreme pressure** is required -- body weight, not just hand strength
4. **Maintain pressure for a minimum of 10 minutes** without lifting or peeking
5. **If cloth/gauze soaks through, do NOT remove it** -- Add more material on top and continue pressing
6. **Support the wound against a firm surface** (ground, bone) when possible

### Why Civilians Fail at Direct Pressure

| Failure Mode | Why It Happens | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Peeking** | Curiosity, wanting to check progress | Disrupts forming clot, restarts bleeding |
| **Insufficient pressure** | Fear of hurting the person, fatigue | Arterial pressure exceeds hand pressure; bleeding continues |
| **Wrong location** | Blood pools away from source; pressing on blood not on wound | No pressure at actual bleed point |
| **Too short duration** | Impatience, thinking "it should have stopped by now" | Clot hasn't formed; bleeding restarts |
| **Removing soaked gauze** | Instinct to replace bloody material | Pulls off forming clot |
| **Single hand, no body weight** | Mimicking TV/movie first aid | Cannot generate sufficient pressure for arterial bleed |

### Can Direct Pressure Alone Stop Arterial Bleeding?

- **Yes, for many wounds** -- especially when the artery can be compressed against underlying bone
- Works well for: temporal artery (scalp), radial/ulnar arteries (wrist/forearm), superficial femoral (mid-thigh against femur)
- Works poorly for: deep arterial injuries, junctional areas (groin, axilla), wounds with large tissue destruction
- **Rule:** If 10 minutes of maximum direct pressure does not control bleeding, escalate immediately

### When to Escalate

- **To tourniquet:** Extremity bleeding (arm or leg) not controlled by direct pressure within 60-90 seconds, or any spurting/pulsatile bleeding from a limb
- **To wound packing:** Junctional wounds (groin, axilla, neck base) where tourniquet cannot be placed
- **Immediately for massive hemorrhage:** Skip direct pressure entirely if blood is spurting or pooling rapidly -- go straight to tourniquet on extremities

## 3. Tourniquets

### Commercial Tourniquets (CoTCCC Recommended)

| Device | Mechanism | Key Feature | Approx. Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CAT (Combat Application Tourniquet)** | Windlass + velcro band | Most widely used, military standard | $25-30 |
| **SOF-T (Special Operations Forces Tactical)** | Windlass + metal buckle | Can be applied one-handed, metal hardware | $25-30 |
| **SAM-XT** | Buckle + auto-lock | Auto-locking mechanism prevents loosening | $30-35 |

**All CoTCCC-recommended tourniquets have 100% effectiveness in studies when properly applied.**

### Commercial vs. Improvised: The Evidence

| Metric | Commercial (CAT) | Improvised (with windlass) | Improvised (no windlass) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Success rate (trained) | ~93-100% | ~45% | ~1% |
| Success rate (untrained) | ~22-62% | Lower | Near zero |
| Failure rate | Very low | **32%** | **99%** |
| Time to apply | Fastest | Significantly slower | Slowest |
| Sustained effectiveness | Excellent | Prone to loosening | Almost never works |

**Key finding:** Improvised tourniquets WITHOUT a windlass have a 99% failure rate. A windlass is absolutely essential.

**Boston Marathon note:** All 27 victims with prehospital tourniquets (many improvised) survived, but trained first responders were present.

### Step-by-Step: Commercial Tourniquet (CAT) Application

1. **Place tourniquet on the limb 2-3 inches ABOVE the wound** (between wound and heart)
2. **If you cannot identify the exact wound location: go HIGH AND TIGHT** -- as high on the limb as possible, into the armpit or groin crease
3. **NEVER place on a joint** (elbow or knee) -- it will not work
4. **Pull the strap tight** and secure through the buckle
5. **Twist the windlass rod** -- keep twisting until bleeding STOPS completely
   - Not until it slows -- until it STOPS
   - You must obliterate the distal pulse (no pulse felt below the tourniquet)
6. **Lock the windlass** in the clip/holder
7. **Mark the time** -- write "TQ" and the time on the patient's forehead, the tourniquet, or tape. Use any marker, pen, blood, mud -- whatever is available
8. **DO NOT LOOSEN** the tourniquet for any reason

### Step-by-Step: Improvised Tourniquet Construction

**Materials needed:**
- **Band/strap:** Any cloth 1.5-2 inches wide and long enough to wrap around limb + tie. Denim strips, torn shirt, scarf, triangular bandage. **NOT elastic, NOT rope, NOT wire, NOT belt (too narrow without windlass)**
- **Windlass:** Rigid stick, sturdy pen, metal spoon, screwdriver, wooden dowel -- 6-8 inches long, must not break under torsion
- **Securing tie:** Another strip of cloth or string to secure windlass after tightening

**Construction and application:**

1. **Wrap the cloth band around the limb** 2-3 inches above the wound (or high and tight if unsure)
2. **Tie a square knot** (right over left, left over right) -- like tying shoes without the bow. Must be snug but allow the windlass to be inserted
3. **Place the windlass (stick) on top of the knot**
4. **Tie another square knot over the windlass** to secure it to the band
5. **Twist the windlass** -- rotate the stick to tighten the band around the limb
6. **Keep twisting until bleeding stops completely**
7. **Secure the windlass** -- tie the loose ends of cloth around one end of the stick so it cannot unwind. If available, use additional material to lock it in place
8. **Check every 5 minutes** -- improvised tourniquets are prone to loosening
9. **If a commercial tourniquet becomes available**, apply it ABOVE the improvised one; do not remove the improvised one first

### Critical Tourniquet Myths -- DEBUNKED

| Myth | Reality | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| "Tourniquets cause amputation" | Tourniquets do NOT cause limb loss. Military data from 20+ years confirms this | Joint Theater Trauma Registry data |
| "Tourniquets are a last resort" | Tourniquets are FIRST-LINE treatment for arterial limb bleeding | TCCC/MARCH protocol |
| "Loosen periodically to restore circulation" | NEVER loosen -- causes renewed bleeding and gradual blood loss | Outdated practice abandoned by all current guidelines |
| "A tourniquet should be comfortable" | A properly applied tourniquet IS PAINFUL. Pain is expected and normal | Royal College of Surgeons |
| "Safe for 8+ hours" | Safe for <2 hours with minimal complications. Muscle damage increases after 2h, approaches irreversibility at ~6h. Case report: limb salvaged after 16 hours | Multiple studies |
| "Belts work as tourniquets" | Belts can slow bleeding but require constant manual pressure and usually fail | Cannot maintain sustained circumferential pressure |

### Tourniquet Pain Management (for voice assistant)

- **Pain is EXPECTED and NORMAL** -- it means the tourniquet is working
- Pain is NOT a reason to loosen the tourniquet
- Loosening due to pain can restart fatal bleeding
- The voice assistant should explicitly say: *"It will hurt. That is normal. That means it is working. Do not loosen it."*
- Patients and bystanders commonly loosen tourniquets due to pain -- this must be preemptively addressed

### Can Untrained Civilians Apply a Tourniquet Successfully?

**Research findings:**
- Untrained layperson with no guidance: **20-22% success rate** with CAT
- With "just-in-time" written instructions: **44% success rate**
- With phone/audio instructions from trained dispatcher: **80.5% success rate**
- After 1-hour training course: **93.4% success rate** with CAT
- **This is exactly the use case for a voice AI assistant** -- guided audio instructions dramatically improve outcomes

### Second Tourniquet

- If bleeding continues after maximum tightening of first tourniquet, apply a **second tourniquet directly above the first**
- Common on muscular limbs (thighs especially) and obese patients
- The voice assistant should be prepared to guide this

## 4. Wound Packing

### When to Pack (Decision Criteria)

Wound packing is for **junctional wounds** -- areas where a tourniquet cannot be placed:
- **Groin / upper thigh crease** (femoral artery junction)
- **Axilla (armpit)** (axillary artery)
- **Base of neck / shoulder junction** (subclavian artery)
- **Buttocks** (gluteal arteries)
- Any deep wound with significant bleeding that is not on an extremity

### When NOT to Pack

- **Chest wounds** -- use occlusive seal instead (packing can worsen pneumothorax)
- **Eye wounds** -- cover, do not pack
- **Head wounds with visible skull fracture** -- cover gently, do not pack into brain cavity
- **Abdominal evisceration** -- do not push organs back in; cover with moist cloth

### Step-by-Step Wound Packing Technique

1. **Expose the wound** -- cut away clothing to see the wound clearly
2. **Identify the bleeding source** -- look for the deepest point where blood is coming from
3. **Take gauze (hemostatic if available, plain if not, or clean cloth)** -- grab a few inches at a time
4. **Push gauze DEEP into the wound** -- use a finger to push it all the way to the bottom, directly against the bleeding vessel
   - This is the most critical step -- the gauze must contact the bleeding source
   - Do not just lay gauze on top of the wound
5. **Continue packing** -- feed more gauze into the wound, packing it tightly with a finger
6. **Fill the entire wound cavity** -- keep packing until absolutely no more gauze fits
7. **Apply firm direct pressure on top** of the packed wound for **minimum 3 minutes** (ideally 5-10 minutes)
8. **If hemostatic gauze: maintain pressure for at least 3 minutes** per CoTCCC guidelines
9. **If bleeding continues after 3 min**, remove packing and repack with fresh gauze (hemostatic if available)
10. **Apply a pressure bandage** over the packed wound if available

### Common Wound Packing Errors

| Error | Why It Fails |
|---|---|
| Packing too shallow / laying gauze on top | Does not reach the bleeding vessel; blood wells up underneath |
| Packing loosely | No tamponade effect; blood flows around gauze |
| Not maintaining direct pressure after packing | Gauze alone cannot stop arterial pressure |
| Removing packing to check | Disrupts clot formation |
| Wicking effect | Dry gauze absorbs blood away from clotting site without stopping flow |
| Not packing enough gauze | A 12-foot roll of combat gauze should be fully used in a large wound |

### Improvised Packing Materials

**Effective:**
- Clean cloth torn into strips (t-shirts, bedsheets)
- Torn clothing (press deep into wound)
- Gauze from any first aid kit
- Menstrual pads can absorb blood but are NOT a substitute for proper packing technique -- they must be pushed INTO the wound, not laid on top

**NOT effective for arterial hemorrhage:**
- **Tampons** -- MYTH. A tampon has ~4 square inches of surface area and absorbs ~9mL of fluid. An arterial bleed can pump 100x that per minute. Tampons are designed to absorb, not to tamponade. They are NOT recommended by any medical authority for wound treatment. The voice assistant should NOT recommend tampons.
- Sanitary pads laid flat on a wound (they wick blood away from clotting site)
- Paper towels (disintegrate when wet)

**The key principle:** Whatever material is used, it must be **packed deep** and **packed tight**, then held with **sustained manual pressure**. The technique matters more than the material.

## 5. Hemostatic Agents

### How They Work

| Agent | Active Ingredient | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **QuikClot Combat Gauze** | Kaolin (clay mineral) | Activates clotting Factor XII, accelerating the coagulation cascade |
| **Celox Gauze** | Chitosan (from shrimp shells) | Binds to red blood cells and platelets, forming a gel-like plug independent of the body's clotting system |
| **ChitoGauze** | Chitosan | Similar to Celox; forms physical seal |

**Key difference:** Celox works even on blood thinned by anticoagulants (warfarin, heparin) because it does not rely on the clotting cascade. This is clinically significant for elderly patients on blood thinners.

### Availability in Ukraine

- **QuikClot Combat Gauze** is available through Ukrainian military retailers (e.g., Abrams.com.ua) for civilian purchase
- **Celox** has been actively distributed in Ukraine through partnerships:
  - Committee to Protect Journalists + Medtrade (Celox manufacturer) partnership supplying journalists
  - Stop the Bleed Coalition kits shipped to Ukraine
  - NATO supply chains (Ukraine receives NATO-standard medical supplies)
- **Civilian access:** Both products are available for purchase online in Ukraine and through volunteer/humanitarian channels
- IFAKs (Individual First Aid Kits) distributed to Ukrainian military and territorial defense forces typically include hemostatic gauze

### Common Hemostatic Agent Mistakes

1. **Laying gauze on top of wound instead of packing deep** -- The agent must contact the bleeding vessel
2. **Not maintaining manual pressure for 3+ minutes** -- Hemostatic agents are NOT magic; they require sustained pressure to work
3. **Hemostatic gauze without pressure is no better than plain gauze** -- Research confirms this
4. **Using expired product** -- Hemostatic agents degrade; check expiration dates
5. **Incomplete packing** -- Must use entire roll in large wounds; a partially packed wound still bleeds
6. **Not repacking on failure** -- If bleeding continues after 3 min of pressure, remove and repack with new hemostatic gauze

## 6. Pressure Points / Indirect Pressure

### Current Status

Pressure points were **removed from AHA/ARC first aid guidelines in 2010** as Class III (risks > benefits). The rationale:
- Difficult for civilians to locate correctly
- Impossible to simultaneously apply direct pressure to wound AND compress a proximal artery
- Tourniquets and wound packing are more effective and reliable

### Recent Research (2022-2023) Challenging This

- Brachial compression achieved complete vessel occlusion in **73% of volunteers**
- Femoral and supraclavicular pressure points were "highly effective" in occluding distal pulses
- Brief instruction improved success to within 5 seconds
- May be reconsidered for guideline re-inclusion

### Practical Guidance for Voice Assistant

**Do NOT teach pressure points as primary technique.** They are:
- Hard to find under stress
- Hard to maintain
- Inferior to tourniquet and packing

**Niche utility only:**
- As a **bridge technique** while someone else prepares a tourniquet
- For **groin/femoral bleeding** while preparing to wound pack: press HARD with fist into the groin crease against the pelvic bone
- The voice assistant could mention: *"While you prepare the tourniquet, press hard with your fist on the inner upper thigh against the bone"*

## 7. Wound-Type Decision Tree

### Arm or Leg Arterial Bleed
**Action:** TOURNIQUET
1. Apply tourniquet 2-3 inches above wound (or high and tight)
2. Tighten until bleeding stops completely
3. Mark time
4. If no tourniquet: improvise with cloth + windlass
5. If no materials for tourniquet: maximum direct pressure with body weight

### Groin / Inner Upper Thigh Bleed
**Action:** WOUND PACKING
1. Expose wound
2. Pack gauze deep into wound against bleeding source
3. Fill entire wound cavity
4. Apply direct pressure on top for 3-5+ minutes
5. Also attempt femoral pressure: press fist hard into groin crease against pelvic bone

### Axilla (Armpit) Bleed
**Action:** WOUND PACKING
1. Pack wound with gauze pushed deep
2. Press arm tight against body to maintain pressure
3. Maintain compression

### Neck Bleed
**Action:** DIRECT PRESSURE (one-sided)
1. Apply firm direct pressure from the SIDE, pressing toward the spine
2. **DO NOT wrap anything around the neck circumferentially** -- this can crush the trachea and obstruct the airway
3. If wound is deep, can carefully pack with gauze while maintaining side pressure
4. Hold pressure continuously until advanced help arrives

### Chest Wound (Penetrating)
**Action:** OCCLUSIVE SEAL (do NOT pack)
1. If commercial chest seal available: apply per instructions (vented seal preferred)
2. If no chest seal: use any airtight material -- plastic wrap, credit card, plastic bag, tape
3. Tape on THREE sides only (leaving one side open as a flutter valve to prevent tension pneumothorax)
4. **Do NOT pack a chest wound** -- this can worsen pneumothorax
5. If actively bleeding from chest wall, apply direct pressure around (not into) the wound

### Scalp Wound
**Action:** DIRECT PRESSURE
1. Scalp wounds bleed profusely due to rich blood supply -- this looks worse than it usually is
2. Apply firm direct pressure with cloth/gauze
3. Wrap head with bandage to maintain pressure if available
4. **Rarely life-threatening** in healthy individuals despite dramatic bleeding
5. Watch for signs of skull fracture (deformity, visible bone, brain matter) -- if present, cover gently, do not press hard

### Abdominal Evisceration (Organs Exposed)
**Action:** COVER, DO NOT PUSH IN
1. **Do NOT attempt to push organs back into the abdomen**
2. Cover exposed organs with moist cloth (wet with clean water if possible)
3. Cover the moist cloth with plastic wrap, a plastic bag, or other water-impermeable material (transparent preferred)
4. Secure gently with tape or bandage
5. Keep the person warm and lying down

## 9. Voice-Assistant Narration Templates

These are structured for a calm, clear voice AI to narrate to a panicked civilian. Each is designed for the specific scenario.

### Template: Extremity Tourniquet (Arm or Leg Spurting Blood)

```
PRIORITY: This is an emergency. I will guide you step by step. Listen carefully and do exactly as I say.

STEP 1: Find the wound. Where is the bleeding? Can you see where the blood is coming from?

[If arm or leg:]

STEP 2: You need a tourniquet. Do you have a first aid kit or tourniquet nearby?

[If YES - commercial tourniquet:]
STEP 3: Take the tourniquet. Place it on the arm/leg ABOVE the wound -- between the wound and the body. Go at least a hand-width above the wound. If you cannot tell where exactly the wound is, place it as HIGH as possible -- near the armpit for an arm, near the groin for a leg.

STEP 4: Pull the strap tight. Now twist the rod -- the windlass. Keep twisting. It must be VERY tight. Twist until the bleeding STOPS completely. Not slows -- STOPS.

STEP 5: It WILL hurt them. That is normal. That means it is working. Do NOT loosen it.

STEP 6: Lock the rod in place. It should click into the clip.

STEP 7: Write the time on their forehead or on the tourniquet. Use anything -- pen, marker, blood. Write TQ and the time.

STEP 8: Do NOT remove or loosen the tourniquet. Leave it until medical help arrives.

[If NO tourniquet available:]
STEP 3: We will make one. Find a piece of cloth -- a shirt, scarf, belt of fabric -- at least as wide as two fingers, and long enough to wrap around the limb and tie.

STEP 4: Find a stick, a pen, a spoon -- something rigid about the length of your hand that will not break.

STEP 5: Wrap the cloth around the limb ABOVE the wound. Tie a simple knot -- like the first step of tying your shoe.

STEP 6: Place the stick on top of the knot. Tie another knot over the stick.

STEP 7: Now twist the stick. Keep twisting. The cloth will get tighter around the limb. Twist until the bleeding STOPS completely.

STEP 8: Tie the ends of cloth around the stick so it cannot unwind.

STEP 9: It WILL hurt. That is normal. Do NOT loosen it.
```

### Template: Wound Packing (Groin/Armpit Bleeding)

```
PRIORITY: The wound is in an area where a tourniquet will not work. I will guide you to pack the wound. This will require you to push material INTO the wound. I know that sounds difficult but it will save their life.

STEP 1: Get gauze, cloth, or a torn piece of clean clothing. You need enough to fill the wound.

STEP 2: Look at the wound. You need to find where the blood is coming from -- the deepest point.

STEP 3: Take the gauze or cloth. Push it DEEP into the wound with your finger. Push it all the way to the bottom where the bleeding is.

STEP 4: Keep pushing more material in. Pack it tight. Fill the entire wound. Do not be gentle -- pack it firmly.

STEP 5: When the wound is completely full and no more material fits, place your hands on top.

STEP 6: Press down HARD with both hands. Use your body weight. Keep pressing.

STEP 7: Do NOT lift your hands to check. Hold pressure for at least five minutes without looking.

STEP 8: Keep pressing until help arrives.
```

### Template: Neck Wound

```
PRIORITY: There is bleeding from the neck. I will guide you. This is important -- do NOT wrap anything around the neck. We must keep the airway open.

STEP 1: Place your hand or cloth directly on the wound. Press firmly toward the SPINE -- press from the side, inward.

STEP 2: Use both hands if needed. Press hard.

STEP 3: Do NOT wrap a bandage around the entire neck. That can block their breathing.

STEP 4: Hold pressure from the side. Do not let go.

STEP 5: Keep pressing until help arrives.
```
